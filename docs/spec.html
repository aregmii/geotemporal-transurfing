Geotemporal Transfusion

A world map with a time control. Move the control to an era and the map fills with the events that happened then; click one and read what it was, and what was happening elsewhere while it did.

Section 01

Three moves make up the whole product

A visitor lands on a world map already populated with events. They do three things, in this order, and nothing else:

  • Move the time control. The map's markers are replaced with events from the newly selected span of years.
  • Look. Marker position says where, marker colour says what kind, marker size says how well known.
  • Click a marker. A panel opens with a picture, a date, a place, a paragraph, a link to the source, and a short list of what was happening elsewhere in the world at the same time.

Everything in this document exists to make those three moves feel good. A feature that does not serve one of them is out of scope for the MVP.

Section 02

The build answers one question: is this worth building properly?

Amish's stated goal is to get the product and the feel right before committing to software. That changes what "done" means. This build is a working prototype used to decide whether the concept holds up, not a v1 of a product with users.

Practically, that means two things for the engineer. First, the data is baked in at build time — there is no server, no database, no incremental update path, and none of those are deficiencies to be fixed later in this scope. Second, the acceptance bar in Section 12 is mostly about responsiveness and coverage, because a laggy or sparse map cannot tell you whether the idea is good.

Definition — build time vs. run time. "Build time" is when a script on a developer machine queries Wikidata and writes a data file. "Run time" is when a person opens the page. In this design all queries happen at build time; at run time the page reads only data that shipped inside it.

Section 04

Era snapping resolves to a sliding window, not a single year

Two things Amish asked for pull against each other. Era snapping means the control moves between named periods. But his own examples are year-precise: 1945 should surface the Second World War; the early 1980s should surface Michael Jackson. A control that only selects "the Modern era" as one 90-year block cannot do that.

The resolution: the control always selects a window — a start year and an end year. The rail is divided into era segments of equal pixel width. Each era declares its own window width in years, wide for antiquity and narrow for the recent past. Dragging inside an era slides the window across that era's range; dragging past an era boundary enters the next era and the window width changes.

92 windows total. The prototype uses wider windows (48 in all) because its placeholder dataset is small; the widths above are the target once the 10,000-event set is in.

Prehistory is a different clock. Before 10,000 BCE the interface says "years ago" rather than BCE, following archaeological convention, and the era labels use Mya and kya. Whether that switch reads as one timeline or two is an open question (Q8).

Requirements

  • REQ-T-1

    The control selects a window [startYear, endYear], never a single year.

  • REQ-T-2

    Era segments are equal width in pixels regardless of the number of years they cover.

  • REQ-T-3

    Dragging snaps to window boundaries; there is no intermediate state where the map shows a half-window.

  • REQ-T-4

    Left and right arrow keys move the window one step. The control is reachable by keyboard tab order.

    The rail is the primary control on the page; it must not be mouse-only.

  • REQ-T-5

    The current era name and year range are always visible without hovering.

  • REQ-T-7

    Segment labels show the year range in primary type with the era name smaller beneath it.

    The eight era names are a European periodization. Subordinating them to the years keeps them useful for navigation without asserting that "Renaissance" describes what was happening in Asia or West Africa.

  • REQ-T-6

    An event whose duration overlaps the window at all is included. A war spanning 1939–1945 appears in both the 1935–1940 and 1940–1945 windows.

Section 06

An event qualifies if it happened somewhere, and ranks by how many languages cover it

The place-anchor rule

The first cut is not "people or not people". It is whether the thing happened at a location a reader can be shown. The Buddha's birth at Lumbini, the crucifixion at Jerusalem, the Salt March at Dandi, the first performance of a symphony at a named hall — all anchor to a place, and all belong on this map. A recording contract, a chart position, or an award has no place worth pointing at, and does not.

This rule does the work the earlier person-based framing was groping for. Modern celebrity milestones fall out of the dataset by themselves: they rarely have a meaningful location, and they rarely have a freely licensed photograph. The founders and turning points do not fall out, because they are attached to real ground.

Ranking

Wikidata contains millions of dated items. Some windows would return tens of thousands of rows; showing them all produces an unreadable map, and picking by hand does not scale to 77 windows. The build needs a ranking signal that is cheap, already in the data, and defensible.

The target is the 10,000 best-known events. The proposed signal is sitelink count — how many language editions of Wikipedia have an article about the item. An event covered in 60 languages is, by any reasonable reading, better known than one covered in two. The Wikidata Query Service exposes this per item; the engineer should confirm the exact predicate before writing the query (see reference 2).

Why not a hand-curated list. Curation gives better quality per event and cannot cover 77 windows across four categories within this scope. The recommendation is to rank automatically, then keep a small manual override file — a list of item IDs to force in or force out — so obvious misses can be corrected without touching the pipeline.

Requirements

  • REQ-E-1

    Rank candidate events within a window by sitelink count, descending.

  • REQ-E-2

    Cap each window at 60 events after ranking.

    60 is the density at which the wireframe in Fig 2 stays readable at 1440px wide. Treat it as tunable, not fixed.

  • REQ-E-3

    Before applying the global cap, reserve up to 5 slots per macro-region (Africa, Americas, Asia, Europe, Oceania) so a window is never entirely European.

    Fill the remaining slots by global rank.

  • REQ-E-4

    Cap any single category at 40% of a window, so a war-heavy period still shows culture and science.

  • REQ-E-5

    Read force-include and force-exclude item IDs from a checked-in overrides file applied after ranking.

  • REQ-E-6

    Drop any event lacking a resolvable date, a resolvable position, or a description of at least 40 words.

  • REQ-E-7

    Include an event only when it anchors to a place a reader can be shown, per the rule above. A person's birth or death is in scope; a person is not.

    In practice this is enforced by the position chain in Section 07 plus REQ-E-6 — an item with no resolvable location is already excluded. No separate person filter is needed.

  • REQ-E-8

    For every event, pre-compute three contextEventIds: the highest-ranked events in the same window that sit on other continents.

    These fill the "elsewhere in the world" list in the panel (REQ-D-5). Computed at build time; the page does no searching.

Known limitation — coverage is uneven. Wikidata's density of dated, positioned items is far higher for Europe and North America and for the last two centuries than for anywhere or anywhen else. REQ-E-3 softens this but cannot fix it. Where a macro-region returns fewer than 3 events for a window, the map should say so in a small note rather than leave an unexplained empty continent.
Section 07

Half the interesting events have no coordinates of their own

Battles and earthquakes carry a coordinate. Events about people usually do not carry one directly — but they carry a route to one: the Buddha's birth points at Lumbini, a first performance points at a hall. The extractor therefore needs a resolution chain that tries progressively weaker sources of position, records which one it used, and drops what never resolves. That last step is also what enforces the place-anchor rule from Section 06.

  • REQ-P-1

    Store a positionConfidence field of exact, place, or associated on every event.

  • REQ-P-2

    Render associated markers hollow and state "location is approximate" in the panel.

  • REQ-P-3

    Jitter markers that resolve to the identical coordinate by up to 8 pixels so they do not stack invisibly.

Section 08

Everything happens in a build script; the page itself does no work

The published page must run with no outbound network calls, because artifacts are served under a content policy that blocks arbitrary hosts. That constraint decides the architecture: a script produces one HTML file, and the file is the product.

The extraction script exists

extract_events.py queries one Wikidata class at a time (wars, battles, treaties, earthquakes, eruptions, pandemics, expeditions, councils, and so on) plus well-known people's births and deaths, ranks by sitelinks, dedupes, and writes the prototype's row format. fetch_summaries.py then replaces Wikidata's one-line descriptions with the lead paragraph of the English Wikipedia article and records the article's lead image URL for the image pipeline. Both cache to disk and are safe to re-run. The class and property identifiers inside were written without access to wikidata.org and must be confirmed on first run.

Query and rate notes for the engineer

  • Endpoint: https://query.wikidata.org/sparql. Individual queries are cut off at a 60-second hard limit, and a client identified by IP and user agent gets 60 seconds of query time per minute, bursting to 120 (references 1 and 3).
  • Send a descriptive User-Agent with contact details; generic agents are throttled (reference 1).
  • 77 windows × 4 categories is 308 queries. Run them sequentially with a delay rather than in parallel, and cache raw responses to disk so a re-run of the ranking step does not re-query.
  • Ask for JSON explicitly via the Accept header; the default response format is XML (reference 1).
Unverified in this document. The property identifiers for coordinate location, point in time, start time, end time and instance-of, and the predicate that exposes sitelink count, are stated from memory and were not confirmed against wikidata.org, which could not be fetched while writing this. Confirm each on its property page before writing the query — reference 2.
Section 09

Images ship inside the file, and photograph the place when they cannot photograph the moment

Images were called core to the feel, so they are in scope. Two facts shape how.

First, delivery. A published artifact cannot load images from an external host at run time, so every thumbnail is downloaded during the build, re-encoded, and written into the HTML as a base64 data URI. At 320 pixels wide and WebP quality 70, a photograph lands near 10 KB; base64 adds roughly a third. Budgeting 300 images gives about 4 MB of image payload, which sits inside the 16 MB artifact limit with room for the basemap and code.

Second, licensing. Commons files are reused under their individual licenses, and reuse outside Wikimedia generally requires naming the author, the license, and the source (reference 4). The build must capture that metadata alongside the image, not afterwards — the imageinfo endpoint returns it in the extmetadata block in the same call that returns the thumbnail URL (references 5 and 6).

The globe's own image

The Earth texture is NASA's Blue Marble, public domain, downscaled to 2048 × 1024 and embedded as a data URI at roughly 300 KB. A credit line stays on screen.

Where event images come from

The place-anchor rule makes this tractable. Freely licensed photographs of twentieth-century people are scarce — album covers and press photos are copyrighted and are not on Commons under a reusable license — but freely licensed photographs of places are abundant, because volunteers photograph sites and monuments. Since every event in this dataset is attached to a place by construction, every event has a candidate image.

The image chain runs in the same order as the position chain: the event's own image, then the image of the place it resolved to. A birth at Lumbini shows Lumbini. A battle shows the battlefield or its monument. This is honest — the caption says what the photograph is of — and it keeps the map visually populated across all four categories.

  • REQ-I-0

    Try the event's own image first, then the image of the place it resolved to. Fall back to the category glyph only when both are missing.

    Label a place image in the panel as the place, never as the event.

  • REQ-I-1

    Embed every thumbnail as a data URI. The page makes no image requests at run time.

  • REQ-I-2

    Store author, license short name, license URL and file page URL with each image, and render them as a credit line in the panel.

  • REQ-I-3

    Skip any file whose license metadata cannot be read. An image without a credit does not ship.

  • REQ-I-4

    Keep total image payload under 5 MB and total page weight under 8 MB.

  • REQ-I-5

    Include a credits view listing every image with its author, license and link.

Section 10

Drawing modern borders under ancient events would be the map's most visible error

The basemap is Natural Earth country vectors, redistributed as TopoJSON and available at three resolutions under an ISC license over public-domain source data (reference 7). Those are today's borders. Placing a 1200 CE event inside the outline of a state founded in 1947 is a claim the product should not make.

Two options:

  • Modern borders throughout, labelled. Cheapest. One line of disclosure. The map keeps saying something false all the way back to 3000 BCE.
  • Borders only where they apply. Render the land-only file for eras before 1900 and switch to the country file for the Industrial era onward. The pre-modern map becomes coastlines and rivers, which is both more honest and visually distinct as you travel back.

My recommendation is the second. It costs one extra basemap file and a conditional, and it converts a factual problem into a feature the user can see happening as they drag.

Decided: the second. Build it that way.

  • REQ-B-1

    Load land-only geometry for eras 1 through 6 and country geometry for eras 7 and 8.

    Cross-fade the border layer at the boundary rather than snapping it, so the change reads as part of the journey rather than as a glitch.

  • REQ-B-2

    Use the 110m resolution file. Higher resolutions cost payload the MVP does not need.

Section 12

Acceptance is measured on responsiveness, coverage and attribution

  • ACC-1

    Moving the window one step repaints the globe in under 100 ms with 10,000 events loaded and up to 400 markers bound.

  • ACC-0

    The globe drags at 60 frames per second on a 2020-or-later laptop with the full dataset loaded.

  • ACC-2

    The page reaches first paint in under 3 seconds on a 10 Mbit/s connection.

  • ACC-3

    Every one of the 77 windows returns at least 12 events. Any window that cannot is listed in the build report.

  • ACC-4

    Every populated macro-region shows at least 3 events per window, or the map shows a sparse-coverage note for that region.

  • ACC-5

    Every displayed image carries author, license and source. A spot check of 20 images finds no missing credit.

  • ACC-6

    Every event panel has a working source link.

  • ACC-7

    The rail is fully operable by keyboard, and the page renders correctly in both light and dark themes.

Section 14

Six questions answered, one still open

Recorded so the engineer knows what was considered and rejected, not only what was chosen.

#QuestionAnswer
Q1Is "Geotemporal Transfusion" the name?Placeholder. Hold it in one constant; renaming through M3 stays a one-line change.
Q2Free photographs of modern people barely exist. What gives?Neither dropping people nor dropping images. Anchor every event to a place, then photograph the place. Celebrity milestones fall out on their own; founders and turning points stay. See Section 06.
Q3Which basemap before 1900?Land only. Country outlines from era 7 onward, cross-faded at the boundary.
Q4Keep the Western era names?Both, ranked. Year range as the label, era name smaller beneath.

Still open

  • Q8

    Does switching from "BCE" to "years ago" at 10,000 BCE read as one timeline or two?

    It follows the convention historians and archaeologists use, and the alternative — "9,500 BCE" for Göbekli Tepe but "68,000 BCE" for the exodus from Africa — is unfamiliar to everyone. Amish to judge on the prototype.

  • Q7

    Do eras 1 and 2 return enough events to be worth entering?

    Deferred to evidence rather than opinion. M0 counts what Wikidata actually returns per ancient window; if a window comes back with four events, the fix is a curated seed list for antiquity, not a shorter slider — the ancient span is wanted.
